01

Observatory Paper

The app is a finely printed almanac. Not a screen pretending to be paper — a sheet with a masthead, hairline rules, tabular figures and one engraved diagram. Celestial by subject matter, never by decoration. Its luxury is the luxury of a well-set table of numbers.

Palette — ink on stock

Impression#211F1BBody ink

Discipline: two inks per sheet plus the brass rule. The brass appears exactly once per screen — on the rule under the masthead — and never as a fill.

Typography

Newsreader — 300 & 400, optical size on

Newsreader (Production Type) for the one sentence that matters. A genuine reading face with optical sizing, drawn for screens; scholarly rather than fashionable. Inter Tight for labels and tabular figures for every time, so columns of numbers align like a timetable. Arabic stays Amiri Naskh, already shipped.

References — what is taken from each

  • HM Nautical Almanac Office — The Nautical AlmanacTimes as a dense, trusted table. Authority with no ornament.
  • Johann Bayer, Uranometria, 1603Engraved celestial plates: line, not glow. Stars as hairline marks.
  • Josef Müller-Brockmann — SBB timetablesGrid as the whole design. Rules doing the work of boxes.
  • Jan Tschichold — Penguin Composition RulesRestraint as a house style, written down and obeyed.
  • Edward Tufte — sparkline pagesA diagram small enough to sit inside a sentence.

How the four dayparts differ

Mechanism: the paper stock and the ink change. Not a tinted background — a different sheet. Morning is warm ivory with sepia-black. Afternoon cools to bone with blue-black. Evening warms to oyster. At night the sheet inverts: ivory ink printed on ink, the way a negative plate reads. Same grid, four substrates.

02

Porcelain & Brass

The app is a precision instrument resting on a porcelain surface. Matte, machined, honest. Nothing glows and nothing is translucent; the only light in the product is the light falling on it. Celestial because a dial is how people have read the sky for six hundred years.

Palette — body, dial, metal

Porcelain#F1EFECBody, day
Dial stone#E7E4DFInstrument face
Midnight ink#191C21Body, night
Brass#B08B55Morning metal
Steel#8E9AA3Afternoon metal
Champagne#C0A173Evening metal

Discipline: one metal visible at a time, and it marks only the thing you must act on. Shadow is a single soft drop — never a glow, never a blur panel.

Typography

Jost 300 · 9:20 PM

Jost (Owen Earl, after Futura) carries the Braun lineage directly — geometric, quiet, excellent at large light weights for a dial reading. IBM Plex Mono for labels and tick marks: designed for machines, and its uppercase at 9–10px is unambiguous at arm's length. Together they look engineered rather than styled.

References — what is taken from each

  • Dieter Rams — Braun AB 20 alarm clockOne dial, one function, zero ornament. The product is the face.
  • Nomos Glashütte — Tangente dialBauhaus watchmaking: thin indices, enormous white space inside a small circle.
  • Naoto Fukasawa — Muji wall CD playerA domestic object that disappears until used.
  • Leica M — engraved body typographySmall type on metal. Confidence at 9px.
  • Teenage Engineering — OP-1 restraintColour used as coding, never as mood.

How the four dayparts differ

Mechanism: the direction and warmth of the light, and which metal it catches. The surface stays porcelain. Morning, light rakes from the left and the metal is brass. Afternoon it comes from overhead, neutral, and the metal cools to steel. Evening it falls from the right, long and warm, and the metal is champagne. At night the body itself goes to midnight ink and the brass becomes the only lit thing on the screen.

Eight hours of opportunity, in two blocks around Fajr.

Orbit's dial, kept and improved: a true 24-hour face. The sector is tonight; the single metal dot is Fajr. Because it is a dial and not a glowing ring, it carries information at a glance without asking to be admired. The sector here is plotted from the real times — 22:05 to 06:30 is 126.2° of the face — not drawn by eye.

Risk: Braun-adjacent minimalism is well-trodden, and the line between instrument and generic Swiss app is thin. It needs the brass and the dial to be genuinely well drawn, or it becomes anonymous.

03

Plaster & Lapis

The app is a lit wall. Lime plaster, a hard-edged shadow, one inlaid medallion. The hour is told by where the light falls and what colour the plaster takes — the way you know the time inside a courtyard without looking at a clock. The celestial reference is architectural: a tiled vault, not a sky photograph.

Palette — plaster, pigment, leaf

Lapis#28446EThe one action

Discipline: the wash is one directional light across plaster — a physical gradient with a reason, not a decorative one. Lapis is reserved exclusively for the primary action, so the eye learns that blue means do this.

Typography

EB Garamond — eight hours, in two blocks

EB Garamond (Octavio Pardo, after Granjon) for everything that is read: a manuscript face with a scholarly register that sits honestly beside Amiri Naskh, the Arabic the app already ships. That pairing is the real argument for this direction — the two faces share a calligraphic ancestry rather than being bolted together. Inter Tight survives only in small caps for labels.

References — what is taken from each

  • Luis Barragán — Casa GilardiColour as light, not as paint. A wall that changes all day.
  • Tadao Ando — Church of the LightOne hard aperture does all the emotional work.
  • Masjid-e Jāmeh, Isfahan — tile vaultsGeometry as devotion; blue reserved, gold sparing.
  • Timurid manuscript illuminationGold leaf as a hairline, never as a fill.
  • Agnes Martin — graphite gridsHand-drawn repetition that stays calm at close range.

How the four dayparts differ

Mechanism: the angle of the cast light and the temperature of the plaster. Morning the wash comes low from the east and the plaster is cool lime. Afternoon it is almost overhead and the wall bleaches. Evening it rakes from the west and the plaster takes ochre. At night the courtyard is unlit — deep blue-black, the medallion visible only by its leaf inlay.

Risk: the most distinctive and the most dangerous. Done badly it is pastiche, and the cultural reference has to be earned rather than borrowed. It also leans hardest on a serif at small sizes, which needs testing on a real iPhone.

My recommendation

03 Plaster & Lapis as the direction, with 02's dial discipline inside it. It is the only one of the three that could not have been generated from a keyword, and the EB Garamond / Amiri pairing is a real argument rather than a mood. Take from 02 the rule that one metal is visible at a time and that the dial is an instrument, not an ornament. 01 is the safest and the least memorable; I would keep its tabular-figure discipline wherever times are listed, and let the rest go.
